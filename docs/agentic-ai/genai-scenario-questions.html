Interview preparation · the project round

GenAI Scenario Questions

61 questions in two halves. First the project deep-dive — the round that opens with "walk me through your most recent project". Then live scenarios: design exercises, incidents, migrations, stakeholder pushback and coding rounds, each written from the interviewer's side so you can see what is being tested. Companion to the Transformers, Embeddings & RAG reference in this folder.

Section 1

Business problem

Answered before any architecture. An engineer who reaches for the stack first signals they were handed a spec rather than involved in shaping one. Keep the whole section to three or four minutes.

1.1

What business problem were you solving?

always first
What is being tested

Whether you can describe the pain without describing the solution. Also whether the problem was real — interviewers hear a lot of projects that existed because someone wanted to use AI.

The structure

One sentence on what people were doing. One on what it cost them — time, errors, rework. One on why it persisted. Stop there; the next four questions unpack it.

Model answer

"Support agents log bugs that customers report, and six engineering teams fix them. The same defect arrives repeatedly, worded completely differently by different customers — so engineers were re-investigating problems that had been diagnosed weeks earlier, and nobody found out it was a repeat until someone happened to remember.

The cost was duplicated investigation time on a queue that was already the bottleneck. It persisted because the only way to find a prior report was to already know it existed."

What loses the room

Starting with "we built a RAG system using LangChain and Pinecone". That answers question 2.1, not this one.

1.2

Who were the users?

core
What is being tested

Whether you know who you built for specifically enough that it shaped a design decision. "Internal users" is not an answer.

The structure

Name each group, what they were doing at the moment the feature appears, and how that changed what you built for them.

Model answer

"Two groups with genuinely different needs. Support agents, at triage — they want 'has this been reported before' in the seconds before they route a ticket, so it has to be instant and it has to be on the page they are already on. Engineers, when picking work up — they want 'what is this and was it fixed before', and they will tolerate a slower, richer answer.

That split is why there are two features rather than one: an always-on panel that costs nothing to render, and an on-demand analysis that costs real money and is only triggered deliberately."

Why that lands

It ties a user distinction to an architectural one. That is the specific thing this question is probing for.

1.3

Why was GenAI required?

the honesty check
What is being tested

Whether you can distinguish a problem that needs semantics from one a well-built index would have solved. This is the question that separates people who chose AI from people who were told to use it.

The structure

Name the property of the problem that conventional software cannot express. Give a concrete pair of examples. Then say what you did not use AI for, which proves the judgement is real.

Model answer

"The core requirement is matching by meaning, not by words. Two reports of the same defect routinely share no vocabulary at all — 'payslip PDF generates blank for contractors' and 'salary statement opens with column headings only' are the same bug and have almost no terms in common. No amount of query tuning, synonym lists or stemming closes that gap, because the similarity is semantic. That is what embeddings are for.

Deliberately not AI: severity and priority stay human-entered, because a model could be argued into P1 by the person reporting the bug; and counting tickets goes through an aggregate query, not retrieval, because that is a database question wearing a natural-language costume."

The strongest single sentence here

"Half of the retrieval is still keyword search, because embeddings are measurably bad at exact tokens like error codes — so the AI is solving the part that needed it and nothing else."

1.4

What was the existing process?

core
What is being tested

Whether you understood the workflow you were changing. Systems that ignore the existing process get built and not used.

The structure

Describe the manual path, name where it broke down, and — importantly — say what about it you kept. Replacing a working step is how adoption dies.

Model answer

"Keyword search over ticket titles, plus institutional memory — someone senior remembering 'we saw this in March'. It worked when the same person handled both reports and failed completely otherwise, which meant the quality of triage depended on who was on shift.

What I kept: the human decision. The system suggests and a person confirms — it never links or closes anything itself. That was partly a trust decision and partly practical, because those confirmations became the only real accuracy signal the system has."

1.5

What measurable improvement did your solution provide?

where people invent numbers
What is being tested

Whether you measured anything at all — and, more sharply, whether you will fabricate a number under mild pressure.

Do not do this

Invent a percentage. The follow-up is always "how did you measure that", and a made-up baseline dies in one question. Interviewers ask this because it is where people embellish.

Model answer — when you have production numbers

"Duplicate-detection rate at triage went from X% to Y% over N weeks, measured as tickets later linked as duplicates that were flagged at creation. Median time to first response on repeat issues dropped from A to B. Suggestion acceptance rate settled around Z%, which is the number I actually watched, because a suggestion nobody accepts is worse than none."

Model answer — when you do not

"I'll be straight: this ran on a synthetic corpus, so I don't have a production before-and-after. What I do have is the measurement the business outcome depends on. On paraphrased duplicates, semantic retrieval finds the right prior ticket at recall@20 of 1.000 against 0.986 for keyword search alone; on error-code queries keyword search gets 1.000 where semantic gets 0.583. That's why the system is hybrid rather than either one.

And I built the acceptance tracking so the business metric is measurable from day one of real use — every suggestion is stored with the relation, the confidence and the model that produced it, so 'are 0.9-confidence duplicates accepted more often than 0.6' is answerable rather than a guess."

Why the second answer still scores well

It gives a real number, names its limits, and shows you designed for the measurement you could not yet take. That reads as product thinking. A fabricated percentage reads as a risk.

Section 2

Architecture

Usually at a whiteboard. The non-AI boxes are what separate a systems engineer from someone who has built a notebook — draw those first.

2.1

Draw the complete architecture.

whiteboard
What is being tested

Whether you see the whole system. Also whether you know that indexing and querying are two different pipelines with different latency budgets.

Draw in this order

1. Boxes and data stores, no arrows. 2. The offline write path. 3. The online read path. 4. Only then name technologies.

Separating the two paths early is the single strongest structural move available in this question.

   client ─► API ─► authZ ─► guardrail ─► retrieve ─► rerank ─► LLM
                                             │                   │

                                                                 │
                                                             response

  ── OFFLINE ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   source ─► ingest ─► chunk ─► embed ─► index
                │                           │
                └──────── reconcile ────────┘

  ── SHARED ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   relational DB (truth) · outbox + worker · traces · usage · feedback
Say this while drawing the shared row

"The relational database is the source of truth and the vector index is derived — I can delete it and rebuild it. Everything else follows: the index holds ids, vectors and filter metadata and no document text, so sensitive content lives in exactly one place to secure, back up and delete from."

2.3

Explain the data flow from user request to final response.

core
What is being tested

Whether you know the shape of the data at each hop, not just the box names. Interviewers probe this by asking "and what exactly comes back from the vector store?"

The trick that makes this answer good

Narrate the data transforming, not the components activating. "A string becomes 1024 floats, which become a list of ids and scores, which become full records via one SQL query."

The detail that shows you built it

"The vector store returns ids and scores — nothing else. The id is the record's primary key, so turning results into displayable rows is one SQL query. That's deliberate: the text is read fresh every time, so an edited record shows its new title immediately, before it's been re-embedded."

2.4

Why was each technology selected?

judgement
What is being tested

Whether you chose or defaulted. The tell is whether you can name the cost of each choice — every real decision has one.

The structure

For each: the reason, then the cost you accepted. A choice with no stated cost sounds like a default.

"The one I'd change is the vector store. pgvector would have given me one store, no dual write and no reconciliation job — and row-level security would have applied to vector search for free, which is a security property rather than a convenience. At my corpus size the dedicated store bought me scale I did not need."

Section 3

RAG

The most heavily weighted block. Seven questions, and the difference between a good and a great answer is whether each stage has a decision attached rather than a description.

3.1

Explain your ingestion pipeline.

core
What is being tested

Whether ingestion is asynchronous and idempotent — the two properties that decide whether it survives contact with production.

Cover these four

What triggers it · how work is queued · how you avoid redundant work · what happens when it fails.

Model answer

"Event-driven, not scheduled. When a record is written, the same transaction writes a row to an outbox table — so the job's existence is atomic with the record's. A separate worker process drains it, claiming with FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED so several workers can run with no broker.

Nothing on the write path calls a model. Creating a record never waits on a GPU and never fails because one is down — if the AI layer is misconfigured, the handler logs and swallows, and reconciliation picks up what was missed.

Idempotency is a content hash over the text plus the model name plus the instruction version. If it matches what's stored, the job ends having cost one indexed query. That's what makes a retry, a duplicate job or a full backfill cheap."

The follow-up they will ask

"Why the model name in the hash?" — "So changing the embedding model invalidates every stored vector automatically, instead of leaving the corpus half in each convention. It turned a model migration into a config change."

3.2

Explain your chunking strategy.

where a strong answer surprises
What is being tested

Whether you understand what chunking is for, or treat it as a mandatory stage with a token count to pick.

The reusable principle

"What is the smallest self-contained thing a user would be satisfied to be shown? Embed that. If it's too small to be found alone, embed the small thing and return its parent."

Model answer — for records

"No chunking, and that's the decision rather than an omission. Chunking exists to fit long documents into a context window and to give retrieval a unit smaller than a whole document. Neither applied: a record is one to two kilobytes and is already the unit a human wants back — returning 'paragraph three of record 1042' helps nobody.

What survives is bounding. Each field has a cap, title first so truncation eats the least important thing, and the caps are part of the content hash so changing one forces a re-embed. I also deliberately excluded three fields: comments, because they change constantly so the vector never settles; resolution notes, because they're written after the problem is understood and would leak the outcome into a triage-time signal; and the customer name, because it clusters by customer instead of by problem."

Model answer — for documents

"Structure-aware first — headings, sections, code functions — because that structure was put there by a human deciding what belongs together, which is exactly the judgement I'd otherwise be reconstructing. Recursive character splitting inside any section that's still too large, with the empty-string separator last so no chunk can ever exceed the limit.

And parent–child where precision and context conflict: embed the small section, return the enclosing document. That dissolves the central tension rather than trading one against the other."

The overlap follow-up

If you use fixed-size with overlap, be ready for: "what does that cost?" Overlap inflates the index by its percentage and puts near-duplicate chunks in your top-k, so three of five results can be three windows over one paragraph. Deduplicate after retrieval.

3.3

Explain embedding and indexing.

core
What is being tested

Two specific things: whether you know your model is asymmetric, and whether your dimension was chosen or inherited.

Cover

Model and why · dimension and why · symmetric or asymmetric · index type · isolation.

Model answer

"A local Qwen3 embedding model at 1024 dimensions, cosine similarity, HNSW index, one namespace per tenant.

Two details worth calling out. First, the model is asymmetric — queries take a task-instruction prefix and documents don't — so the interface has separate embed_query and embed_documents methods. Getting that wrong produces no error at all, just measurably worse recall, so I made it structurally impossible rather than a convention.

Second, the dimension is probed from the model at startup, not configured. I started on the 8B variant at 4096 dimensions, which was never a decision — it was the default the model emitted. 4096 is four times the storage, four times the index memory and four times the distance computation. The 0.6B variant emits 1024 natively, so switching was one config line, and because the model name is in the content hash every stored vector invalidated automatically."

If they ask how you'd validate the dimension choice

"Embed at full dimension, truncate to 2048 / 1024 / 512, run the retrieval eval at each. Recall@k is free to compute. If quality is flat at 1024, take the 4× saving. Many models are Matryoshka-trained, so truncation is valid — you just have to re-normalise afterwards."

3.4

Explain retrieval.

core
What is being tested

Whether you understand the funnel — that there is no single "top K" but a sequence of narrowing steps with different costs.

The governing principle to state

"A re-ranker can reorder what it is given but cannot recover a document retrieval never returned. So the pool's job is recall, and precision at the head is the re-ranker's."

  corpus
    ├── dense  top 50 ─┐   over-fetched: exclusions removed AFTER the search
    ├── sparse top 50 ─┤
    │                  ▼
    │            fuse + dedupe
    │                  ▼   20 candidates      ← the re-ranker's menu
    │                  ▼   score floor
    │                  ▼   10 re-ranked       ← cost lives here
    │                  ▼   confidence floor
    └──────────────────▼    5 shown
Model answer

"Two retrievers run concurrently — the framework places them in the same superstep — each returning 50, over-fetched because excluded ids are removed after the search and would otherwise eat into the limit. Fusion de-duplicates to 20 candidates, and only then is a score floor applied.

That ordering matters: the floor is applied after fusion, not per retriever, because a document ranked 40th by both retrievers is a better candidate than one ranked 8th by a single retriever — and a per-retriever cutoff would have discarded it before fusion ever saw the agreement.

One thing that surprises people: the query is embedded live at request time. Nothing looks up the subject record's stored vector — the record you're looking at is the query, and the stored vectors are the corpus. That's why the feature works on a record created one second ago that has never been embedded."

3.5

Explain hybrid search.

bring numbers
What is being tested

Whether "we used hybrid" is a slogan or a measured decision. The strongest possible answer contains two numbers pointing in opposite directions.

The argument to make

Not "two is better than one". It is: neither wins everywhere, and which one wins changes with the query — so deleting either loses a whole query family.

Model answer

"Dense and sparse, both always run. I measured them separately on two query families and they invert: on paraphrased duplicates, semantic gets recall@20 of 1.000 and keyword 0.986. On error-code queries, keyword gets 1.000 and semantic 0.583. Delete either and you lose a family.

The reason is tokenization. An identifier like ERR-40001 fragments into meaningless subword pieces, so its embedding is not usefully close to anything — embeddings are systematically bad at exact tokens. That's the whole case for keeping keyword search, and it's why I report retrieval metrics per query family rather than averaged: an average is exactly the shape that would have hidden this."

Have this bug ready — it is the best challenge answer in RAG

"Postgres tokenizes ERR-40001 as 'err' plus '-40001' — the hyphen stays with the number. My Python-side query builder stripped hyphens and searched 40001, which appears in no document. Every error-code lookup had been silently returning nothing, and it read as 'keyword search is just weak here'. The fix was to build the tsquery in SQL so the query and the index are analysed by the same tokenizer and cannot disagree. Recall@5 went from 0.083 to 0.625."

3.6

Explain re-ranking.

core
What is being tested

Whether you know why a re-ranker can do something retrieval cannot — and whether you can justify its latency cost.

The mechanism to state

Retrieval scores query and document separately, which is what makes it indexable and what makes it approximate. A re-ranker scores them together, so it can model interaction — at one forward pass per candidate.

Model answer

"Top 10 candidates only, because cost scales linearly with them. I used an LLM rather than a cross-encoder, and that needs justifying: a cross-encoder gives you a better order, but the panel needed a label with a reason — duplicate, recurring, or related. 'Someone is already working on this' and 'this was fixed and has come back' route to completely different human responses, and a score can't express that.

It's off by default and degrades to the fused order if the model is unavailable — which is correct rather than a fallback, because the fused results are what shipped before re-ranking existed and they were measured to be good.

Measured: precision@5 went from 0.275 to 0.635 for about 3.5 seconds — and it halved the list rather than padding it. A panel showing two good matches beats one showing five of which three are noise."

A detail that shows care

"The prompt carries each candidate's status and closure date, because 'recurring' isn't a judgement about wording — it's a judgement about a closed item coming back, and the model can't make it without knowing the item was closed."

3.7

How did you evaluate retrieval quality?

the differentiator
What is being tested

Whether you can tell a regression from noise — and whether your evaluation is cheap enough to actually run.

Lead with this

It costs zero LLM calls. recall@k, MRR and nDCG are arithmetic over a ranked list, so they can gate every commit the way unit tests do.

Model answer

"A golden set of labelled pairs, scored with recall@k, MRR and nDCG, reported per query family rather than averaged. Every configuration runs through the shipped code path selected by a setting — an ablation with its own copy of the algorithm measures the copy, and the two drift apart the first time one is edited.

It's free and deterministic, so it gates every change and exits non-zero if the shipped fusion falls below a plain single retriever. That's the one outcome worth failing a build over."

The story that beats any metric — an audit of your own benchmark

"My first benchmark was worthless and looked perfect. Every configuration scored 1.000 on every metric, which reads as success and actually means the benchmark has no power to distinguish anything. The cause: the golden variants paraphrased only the titles and reused the bodies, so the task had become exact-text matching, which both retrievers solve perfectly.

I found the same fault one level out later — the surrounding corpus was drawn by weighted sampling, so most of it sat in exact-duplicate groups. Rebuilding it kept every conclusion and collapsed the margins: the case for embeddings on paraphrase went from +0.158 to +0.014, a single query. A saturated benchmark reads as success, which is exactly what makes it dangerous."

And the caveat to volunteer

"The labels are synthetic and I wrote both the system and the ground truth, so I read those numbers comparatively rather than as production accuracy. What the harness genuinely gives me is the ability to tell whether a change helped, for free, on two query families."

Section 4

Agentic AI

The tell for whether you have run an agent unattended is simple: do you talk about limits and failure before you talk about capabilities?

4.1

Explain your agent architecture.

core
What is being tested

Whether you built an agent or a workflow you are calling an agent. The distinction: does the model decide the control flow, or do you?

Cover

The pattern and the one you rejected · what the loop does per turn · what is deterministic rather than model-decided.

Model answer

"ReAct — reason, act, observe, repeat — with the model deciding each step from what it just learned. It runs in a worker rather than on a request, because latency and cost are unbounded by nature and that doesn't belong on a page render.

One implementation choice worth mentioning: tool calls go through structured output rather than provider-native function calling. Each turn the model returns a decision object — a tool with arguments, or finish. That's what lets one interface serve both a hosted model and a local one identically, which is the only reason the fallback chain works at all. An agent built on native tool calling can't fall back to a model that lacks it.

And the first step is not model-decided: a deterministic priming node fetches the subject record before the model gets a turn, so the first observation always exists."

Why priming exists — tell this

"Given an empty observation list, the agent wrote a fluent, confident, entirely invented report. The failure wasn't being wrong — it was being wrong from nothing. Priming makes that structurally impossible rather than asking the prompt to prevent it."

4.2

Why single-agent or multi-agent?

over-engineering trap
What is being tested

Restraint. Many candidates assume multi-agent is the more impressive answer. Defending single with a clear rule is usually stronger.

The rule to state

Default to one agent with more tools. Split only on: a permission boundary, different models, context pressure from too many tools, genuine parallelism, or separate team ownership.

Model answer — defending single

"Single, deliberately. Seven read-only tools and one reasoning loop. I checked it against the reasons to split and none applied: no permission boundary — every tool is read-only over the same scoped session; no need for different models per role; seven tools is well inside what a model selects accurately; no genuine parallelism, because each step depends on the last; and one owner.

Multi-agent would have added lossy hand-offs — every boundary is a summarisation — serial latency, multiplied cost, and non-local failures where agent C fails because agent A misphrased something two hops back. That's a real cost for no benefit I could name."

Then show you know when to split

"Where I would split is a permission boundary. If part of the work needed write access, I'd make that a separate component that only executes approved actions and makes no decisions — so every agent that reasons stays read-only, and a prompt injection can mislead the reasoning but can't move anything. That's a structural guarantee rather than a behavioural one."

4.3

Explain agent communication.

core
What is being tested

Whether you know that in a graph framework agents and nodes do not message each other — they read and write shared state.

If you have a single agent

Say so, and describe node-to-node communication instead. Do not invent agent-to-agent messaging you did not build.

Model answer

"There isn't agent-to-agent communication — it's a single agent, so what matters is how nodes communicate, and they do it through shared graph state rather than by calling each other.

Each node is a function of state that returns a partial dict; the framework merges it. No node mutates shared state directly, which is what makes each one testable with a plain dictionary and no framework at all.

Where it gets interesting is concurrency. Two retriever nodes run in the same superstep and both write to the same key — so that key needs a reducer that merges by source. Without one, the default is overwrite and you silently lose one retriever's results, which presents as 'the keyword retriever is broken'."

If they push on multi-agent communication

"In a supervisor pattern the hand-off is still state, not messages — the supervisor writes a task into state and reads back a result. The design question is what gets summarised at each boundary, because every hand-off is lossy and that loss is where multi-agent systems actually fail."

4.4

Explain state management.

the differentiator
What is being tested

Reducers, and whether you have hit the problem they solve. This is the question that separates people who read the docs from people who shipped.

Cover four things

What is in state · how concurrent writes merge · what resets and what accumulates · what survives a restart.

Model answer

"A typed state object with a reducer per key, because the default is overwrite and that is wrong for most fields.

Overwrite for scalars like the query. Append for the trace, because the sequence is the audit trail. Merge-by-source for retriever results, because two nodes write that key concurrently. And for the assistant's working memory, a custom reducer — observations must reset each turn, since evidence from ten minutes ago isn't evidence for the current question and carrying it grows every prompt for the life of the thread. Plain append can't express 'reset', so it takes a sentinel value the reducer recognises.

Durability is separate: conversation state is persisted per thread by a Postgres checkpointer after every superstep, so nothing in the process has to still be alive. That's what makes a conversation survive a deploy."

A detail that proves you built it

"Dependencies — ports, model registry, limits — go in context, not state. Putting a database session in state would make it something reducers merge and the checkpointer tries to serialise."

4.5

Explain tool calling.

core
What is being tested

Validation and blast radius. The strongest thing you can say is not which tools exist but which verbs do not.

Cover

How the model expresses a call · how arguments are validated · what tools can and cannot do · what a result looks like.

Model answer

"Seven tools, all read-only. There is no write tool — not a disabled one, none exists. That's the strongest guarantee in the system because it's structural rather than behavioural: a successful prompt injection can mislead the reasoning but still cannot change data.

Arguments come from a language model, so they're validated by a schema at the boundary — a model will eventually send a limit of 100,000 or a status value that's actually an injection attempt, and every one of those becomes a clean rejection the agent can read and retry rather than a query that runs.

And tool results describe themselves. If a filter was coerced away, the result says which filters were ignored — otherwise the query silently widens and the model reasons confidently about a set it thinks was filtered."

Two bugs worth having ready

Descriptions never reaching the model. Only parameter names were being sent, so the agent kept choosing wrong tools — which read as "the model is weak", and the tools were redesigned twice before anyone checked what the model actually received. Log the exact prompt before tuning the prompt.

Schema and model disagreeing. Asked "which items are open", the model sent a bare string where the schema demanded a list. The agent honestly reported "the query had an invalid format" — a true sentence, useless to the asker. Coercing a natural model output stops it being an error.

4.6

Explain failure handling.

the production question
What is being tested

Whether you have run an agent with nobody watching. The answer should be about bounds, and it should be clear the model cannot talk past them.

The principle

Multiple independent limits, each catching what the others miss, all routing to the same terminal step so a partial result is still written.

Model answer

"Four independent limits plus repeat detection. A turn cap for a model that won't stop reasoning. A wall clock, because the turn cap never fires if a provider call never returns. A tool-call cap for fan-out without convergence. A cost cap for an expensive loop nobody is watching. And signature-based repeat detection for the same call with near-identical arguments, forever.

They're independent because each catches something the others don't — and none of them lives in the prompt, because a limit the model is merely told about is a suggestion.

Critically, every limit routes to the same reporting step. A run that exhausts its budget still persists what it learned, flagged partial with a stop reason, rather than vanishing."

The failure state people forget

"A run that crashed mid-flight left its row marked running and blocked its subject forever. I added a staleness sweep at twice the timeout. The general lesson: a state machine needs a way out of every state — including the ones you reach by dying."

4.7

Explain human-in-the-loop.

enterprise favourite
What is being tested

Which of the four patterns you used — suggest, approve, escalate, review — and whether the loop is closed, meaning the human's verdict becomes data.

The technical requirement

If a human gates execution, the workflow must pause and resume across a process restart. A person may take a day. That means durable state, not an in-memory loop.

Model answer

"Suggest-and-confirm — the weakest-friction pattern, chosen because the system proposes rather than acts, so nothing needed gating. Every suggestion carries agree and reject controls.

The important part is that the loop is closed. A verdict is stored with the relation, the confidence and the model that produced it — which is what makes calibration answerable later: are 0.9-confidence suggestions accepted more often than 0.6-confidence ones?

Two design details. Re-running the pipeline deletes only pending suggestions — a human's judgement is data paid for with someone's attention and survives a model change. And a pair someone already rejected is never re-suggested, because re-suggesting a rejected pair is the fastest way to teach people to ignore the panel.

The feature and its own evaluation dataset are the same thing."

If they ask about approval gating

"For anything that writes, spends or sends, I'd use approve rather than suggest — the graph interrupts before the executing node, persists, and resumes when the approval arrives, possibly in a different process hours later. That's exactly what a checkpointer is for."

Section 5

Production

The block where a project that never met real traffic becomes obvious — usually by the third follow-up, not the first. If that is your situation, 5.1 has the answer that holds up.

5.1

How did you deploy the system?

do not bluff this
What is being tested

Whether it shipped. Deployment claims are the easiest thing in the interview to test — "which ingress?", "how did a rollout interact with an in-flight agent run?", "what did p99 look like?"

If it did ship

If it did not

Three moves: concede in one sentence · redirect to the production-shaped engineering that is real · name the operational gap specifically. Never describe a deployment that did not happen.

Model answer — deployed

"Containerised, on Kubernetes. Two deployments from one image: the API and the worker, separate because they scale on different signals — the API on request rate, the worker on queue depth — and because a slow embedding job must never make the API unready.

"I should be straight with you: this one isn't deployed. It runs locally against a real database and a managed vector store. I built it to be production-shaped rather than having operated it in production, and I'd rather tell you which parts of that are actually real.

5.4

How did you control latency and cost?

deep
What is being tested

Whether cost was designed in or bolted on. The best answer starts with architecture, not with a rate limit.

The framing

Cost lives in the topology — how many model calls a request makes by design — and only then in caps and caching.

Model answer

"Cost is in the topology first. Blocked input costs zero calls. Good retrieval with re-ranking off costs zero. With re-ranking on, one. Worst case with the corrective rewrite loop, three. On a constrained quota those numbers are the design, not a footnote — and the grading step that decides whether to escalate deliberately uses counts rather than a model, because asking an LLM 'are these results good' spends a call to answer a question two integers already answer.

Then the controls: a model registry keyed by role rather than model name, so swapping which model serves a role is configuration. Per-role daily caps, per-user rate limits, a per-tenant monthly ceiling. An ordered fallback chain from hosted-strong to hosted-cheap to local.

And caching keyed on the record's version rather than a TTL — the optimistic-locking counter already increments on every content change, so an edited record gets a new key automatically and a stale result can never be served."

Two accounting bugs worth telling

Pricing by role instead of by the model that answered. When a fallback dropped to the local model, the run was still billed at the hosted rate — inventing money. Cost per accepted result is only meaningful if the costs are real.

Daily budgets counted per model instead of per role. Two roles shared a model, so the busy one silently exhausted the other's budget and it stopped running — with no error anywhere.

The metric to name

"Cost per successful task, not cost per call. Cost per call rewards making calls cheaper; cost per success rewards making them useful — and a cheaper model that fails twice as often moves it the wrong way."

5.5

How did you handle failures?

core
What is being tested

Whether an AI outage takes down the product. The right instinct is that AI features degrade; they do not fail the request.

The rule

Decide per component whether a failure is fatal or degrading — and make degradation visible rather than silent.

Model answer

"Layered, with degradation as the default rather than the exception.

If the vector store is unreachable, the semantic retriever returns an empty ranking rather than raising — the keyword half still works, and a partial result beats no result on a page a human is already reading. If the re-ranking model is unavailable, the fused order stands, which is correct rather than a fallback, because the fused results are what shipped before re-ranking existed.

If the AI layer is entirely down, the product still works — readiness deliberately doesn't depend on it, because no AI call sits on a write path. A record can always be created.

And degradation is reported, not hidden: the response carries a flag computed from the execution trace, so the UI can say 'one search was unavailable' rather than quietly showing worse results. A response that silently came from half the pipeline is a lie of omission."

The ingestion half

"On the write path, event handler failures are logged and swallowed on purpose — a record must never fail to be created because the AI layer is misconfigured. The reconciliation job exists precisely to pick up what was missed, which is what makes swallowing safe rather than negligent."

Section 6

Evaluation

Usually the last block, and often where the interview is decided — because most candidates say "we used RAGAS" and stop.

6.1

How did you evaluate RAG?

core
What is being tested

Whether you separate retrieval from generation. They fail differently and fixing the wrong one is the most common wasted week in RAG work.

The structure

Two questions, two answers, two cadences. "Is it retrieving the right information" is free and gates every change. "Is it generating a reliable answer from it" costs money and runs occasionally.

Model answer

"Separately, because the diagnosis differs. Retrieval: recall@k, MRR and nDCG over a golden set, reported per query family rather than averaged, costing zero model calls — so it gates every commit the way unit tests do, and exits non-zero if the shipped fusion falls below a plain single retriever.

Generation: faithfulness, answer relevancy and context precision via RAGAS, which needs an LLM judge, so it runs on demand rather than on every push.

The separation is what makes it diagnostic. High retrieval with low faithfulness means the model is ignoring good context — a prompt or model problem. Low retrieval with high faithfulness means it's faithfully reporting that it found nothing — a retrieval problem. Those need completely different fixes."

6.2

How did you evaluate generation quality?

deep
What is being tested

Whether you know the metrics' limitations. Anyone can list them; the signal is knowing which one to gate on and which one to distrust.

Cover

The three RAGAS metrics and what each asks · which one gates · the two traps.

Model answer

"Three metrics. Faithfulness — is every claim in the answer supported by the retrieved context? That's the hallucination detector and the one I gate on. Answer relevancy — does it address the question asked? Context precision — how much of what was retrieved was actually useful, which is really a retrieval signal read from the generation side.

Two limitations I'd flag about my own numbers. The judge is unstable — I scored one identical sample three times at temperature 0 and got 0.25, 0.25, 1.00. So I read these as a trend across runs, never as a per-row verdict.

And relevancy punishes correct refusals. RAGAS classifies noncommittal answers as irrelevant by design, so 'the data doesn't answer that' scores 0.00 — which is good behaviour being marked down. Optimising that number would reward guessing, which is exactly why faithfulness is the gate."

The line that shows judgement

"Knowing which metric to ignore is as useful as knowing which to optimise. I'd rather ship a system that refuses honestly and scores 0.6 on relevancy than one that guesses confidently and scores 0.9."

6.3

How did you measure hallucination?

deep
What is being tested

Whether you have a cheap deterministic layer, or only an LLM judge. The cheap layer catches the most damaging class.

Three layers, cheapest first

Deterministic citation grounding · claim-level LLM judging · NLI entailment.

Model answer

"Layered. The cheapest catches the worst class: deterministic citation grounding. Extract every reference the model claimed, check each against what was actually retrieved. No model needed, and it runs on every response — a fabricated identifier is uniquely corrosive because it's specific, it looks checkable, and the reader only finds out after clicking.

Above that, claim-level judging via RAGAS faithfulness, which decomposes the answer into atomic claims and checks each against the context."

The complete story to tell — a hallucination diagnosed and fixed

"An evaluation run scored one answer faithfulness 0.20 with relevancy 0.88 — the dangerous combination: fluent, on-topic and unsupported. The model had answered a count correctly and then decorated it with three invented record references.

The cause was precise once I looked at the context: the aggregate tool returned counts with no references at all, and the model filled the absence. Two fixes — make the tool declare explicitly that it carries no references, and change the guardrail to strip fabricated citations used as decoration rather than merely flagging them. Faithfulness went from 0.73 to 0.92.

The generalisable lesson: a model fills an absence with invention, so a tool result has to describe its own shape — including what it doesn't contain."

6.4

How did you evaluate agents?

deep
What is being tested

Whether you evaluate the trajectory or only the output. Agents fail in ways a final-answer check cannot see.

The distinction

RAG evaluation scores an answer. Agent evaluation has to score the path — did it call the right tools, in a sensible order, without flailing.

Model answer

"Differently from RAG, because the output isn't the whole story. Four things:

Task success rate against a fixed set of tasks with checkable outcomes. Tool-call success rate, which is the best early warning — it degrades before answers visibly do, and it separates failure classes that need different fixes: invalid arguments mean schema and model disagree; wrong tool chosen means descriptions are ambiguous; empty results mean the problem is upstream.

Turns per task, because a rising number means the agent is flailing even when it eventually succeeds. And the step log — every tool call in order — because 'why did it miss the obvious answer' is unanswerable from the output alone but obvious from the trajectory: you can see it searched once with a poor query and never retried.

Structural red-team checks sit alongside these: assertions about the system rather than the model — that no write tool is registered, that every tool takes a scoped session. Those are deterministic and free, and unlike a prompt they can't regress silently."

Section 8

Live design exercises

You are given a situation and asked to design on the spot. The single biggest signal here is whether you ask questions before you draw. A candidate who starts sketching immediately has told the interviewer they design without requirements.

The five questions to ask in every design scenario

1. Scale — how many documents, how many queries per second? 2. Latency — is this interactive or batch? 3. Accuracy stakes — what happens when it is wrong? 4. Data — where does it live, who may see it, how often does it change? 5. Constraints — budget, cloud, compliance, existing stack.

Ask three or four of these, then design. It costs ninety seconds and it changes the design — which is the point.

8.1

"Design a RAG system for our legal team to search 200,000 contracts."

whiteboard
What I am testing as the interviewer

Whether you notice that legal is a precision-critical, citation-mandatory domain and that contracts have structure worth using. If you propose 512-token fixed chunks I know you have one template.

What I want you to ask me first

"Are these searchable by clause, or by whole agreement?" · "Do answers need to cite the exact clause?" · "How often do contracts change — is it append-only?" · "Who can see which contracts?"

A strong answer, once you have the answers

"Given clause-level questions and mandatory citation, the retrieval unit is the clause and the design follows from that.

Chunking: structure-aware on clause numbering, not token counts — contracts are already numbered by lawyers who decided what belongs together. Parent–child: embed the clause, return the clause plus its section heading and the parent agreement's metadata, because a clause without its definitions section is often meaningless.

Retrieval: hybrid, and keyword matters more here than usual — legal language is full of defined terms, party names and section references where exact matching beats semantics. Metadata filters on jurisdiction, counterparty, effective date and contract type, because half of legal search is 'in our Delaware agreements signed after 2022'.

Generation: every claim cited to a clause, and citations validated against what was retrieved rather than trusted. In this domain an ungrounded citation is not an inconvenience, it is malpractice — so I would rather return 'I can't find this' than a plausible answer.

Access control: pre-filtered by the caller's permissions at query time, never post-filtered. Contracts are exactly the data where seeing the wrong one is the incident."

The follow-up I would ask

"A lawyer asks 'what's our standard indemnity cap?' and gets five different answers from five contracts. Averaging across contracts is how a RAG system produces confident legal fiction.

"Legal says no customer data may leave our network. Redesign."

What I am testing

Whether you find all the egress points. Most candidates name the LLM and stop — there are at least five.

The list I want

Strong answer

"First I'd enumerate every egress point rather than assume it's just the LLM. Then, in order of difficulty:

"Users say the answers got worse this week. Nothing was deployed. Go."

9.2

"Our LLM bill tripled overnight. Find it."

Whether you instrumented cost by dimension. If your usage data only has a total, you cannot answer this and I will find that out in thirty seconds.

9.3

"A customer says the assistant showed them another customer's data."

10.1

"You need to change the embedding model. There are 40M vectors in production."

the migration question
What I am testing

Whether you know vectors from two models are not comparable. If you propose re-embedding in place while serving, you have just described a system returning nonsense for hours.

The fact everything follows from

Two embedding spaces have no meaningful geometric relationship. A query embedded with model B compared against vectors from model A produces confident garbage — not degraded results, meaningless ones.

Strong answer

"Build alongside, never in place. Two embedding spaces aren't comparable, so a partially-migrated index isn't degraded — it's incoherent.

I'd rate-limit it so it doesn't starve production traffic of embedding throughput. The follow-up

"Can you avoid the re-embed by keeping both and querying both?" — You can, and it costs you two searches per query and a fusion problem between incomparable score scales. It's a reasonable transitional state during a canary, not a destination.

10.2

"Your corpus is about to grow from 50k documents to 20M. What breaks first?"

What I am testing

Strong answer

"The first thing that breaks isn't technical — it's my evaluation. At 50k with exact search, retrieval recall is 1.0 by construction, so my golden set was measuring the embedding model. 12.1

"Write a chunker with overlap."

very common

Edge cases to name before writing: overlap ≥ size (infinite loop), empty input, text shorter than one chunk, and whether the final chunk should be padded or short.

def chunk(text: str, size: int, overlap: int = 0) -> list[str]:
    """Split text into overlapping windows.

    Raises on overlap >= size, because the stride would be zero or
    negative and the loop would never terminate.
    """
    if size <= 0:
        raise ValueError("size must be positive")
    if overlap >= size:
        raise ValueError("overlap must be smaller than size")
    if not text:
        return []

    step = size - overlap
    return [text[i:i + size] for i in range(0, len(text), step)]

Complexity: O(n) time and O(n) output — though note the output is larger than the input by the overlap fraction, which is worth saying out loud because it is the practical cost.

The follow-up I would ask

"Now don't split mid-sentence." — The move is to snap the boundary backwards to the last sentence terminator within the window, and to fall back to a hard cut if there isn't one, so a pathological input can't produce an oversized chunk. 

def chunk_on_sentences(text: str, size: int, overlap: int = 0) -> list[str]:
    chunks, start = [], 0
    while start < len(text):
        end = min(start + size, len(text))
        if end < len(text):
            cut = max(text.rfind(". ", start, end),
                      text.rfind("\n", start, end))
            if cut > start:            # only if we actually found one
                end = cut + 1
        chunks.append(text[start:end].strip())
        start = max(end - overlap, start + 1)   # guarantee progress
    return [c for c in chunks if c]

start + 1 in that last line is the detail worth pointing at — without it, a window with no sentence break can fail to advance.

12.2

"Merge several ranked lists into one."

fusion in disguise

This is Reciprocal Rank Fusion, and recognising that out loud is worth points on its own.

from collections import defaultdict

def fuse(ranked_lists: list[list[str]], k: int = 60) -> list[str]:
    """Reciprocal Rank Fusion.

    Uses rank rather than score because scores from different retrievers
    are not comparable -- a cosine of 0.72 and a BM25 score of 8.4 live
    on unrelated scales, but "third" and "third" do not.
    """
    scores: dict[str, float] = defaultdict(float)
    for ranked in ranked_lists:
        for rank, doc_id in enumerate(ranked, start=1):
            scores[doc_id] += 1.0 / (k + rank)
    return sorted(scores, key=lambda d: -scores[d])

Complexity: O(N log N) on the number of distinct documents, dominated by the sort.

What earns extra credit

Explaining what k does: it dampens the difference between top ranks. At k=60, rank 1 scores 0.0164 and rank 2 scores 0.0161 — nearly equal. A small k makes the top position dominate; a large k flattens everything toward equal weighting. Then adding: "and if the retrievers are of unequal quality for a given query, that flattening is a problem, which is why RRF sometimes loses to just taking the better retriever's order."

12.3

"Pack documents into a token budget, best first."

context assembly

The judgement call to raise: when the next document doesn't fit — stop, skip and continue, or truncate it? Say which you'd choose and why.

def pack(docs: list[Doc], budget: int, count_tokens) -> list[Doc]:
    """Fill a context budget with the highest-ranked documents that fit.

    Skips a document that doesn't fit and keeps trying smaller ones,
    rather than stopping at the first overflow -- that recovers budget
    a single large document would have wasted.

    Never truncates. Half a document reads as complete and is often
    worse than omitting it, because the model can't tell it was cut.
    """
    chosen, used = [], 0
    for doc in docs:                      # assumed already ranked
        cost = count_tokens(doc.text)
        if cost > budget:
            continue                      # too big for ANY budget; skip
        if used + cost > budget:
            continue                      # doesn't fit now; a later one may
        chosen.append(doc)
        used += cost
    return chosen
The follow-up

"Does order matter after packing?" — Yes, and it's worth knowing: models attend most reliably to the beginning and end of their context, so material in the middle is effectively weaker. With many documents, placing the strongest at both ends rather than in rank order is a real technique.

        return []
    return heapq.nlargest(k, scores, key=scores.get)


# By hand, if asked to implement it -- a MIN-heap of size k, so the
# smallest of the current best is always at the root and cheap to evict.
def top_k_manual(scores: dict[str, float], k: int) -> list[str]:
    heap: list[tuple[float, str]] = []
    for doc_id, score in scores.items():
        if len(heap) < k:
            heapq.heappush(heap, (score, doc_id))
        elif score > heap[0][0]:
            heapq.heapreplace(heap, (score, doc_id))
    return [doc_id for _, doc_id in sorted(heap, reverse=True)]
The counter-intuitive bit to explain

"It's a min-heap even though we want the largest values — because the thing you need constant access to is the weakest member of the current best k, so you can evict it. That inversion is the whole trick."

practical

A real problem when chunks overlap: three of your five results can be three windows over the same paragraph. Ask which definition of "near-identical" they want — exact, textual, or semantic — because the answers differ sharply in cost. 

def dedupe(results: list[Result], threshold: float = 0.9) -> list[Result]:
    """Drop later results that substantially repeat an earlier one.

    Greedy and order-dependent on purpose: results arrive ranked, so the
    first occurrence is the best-scoring one and is the one to keep.

    Jaccard over token sets -- cheap, no model, and good enough for the
    overlapping-window case this exists to solve. Semantic near-duplicates
    with different wording need embeddings, which costs a call.
    """
    kept: list[Result] = []
    seen_tokens: list[set[str]] = []

    for result in results:
        tokens = set(result.text.lower().split())
        if not tokens:
            continue
        if any(
            len(tokens & prior) / len(tokens | prior) >= threshold
            for prior in seen_tokens
        ):
            continue
        kept.append(result)
        seen_tokens.append(tokens)
    return kept

Complexity: O(n²) in the number of results — fine for 20 candidates, wrong for 20,000. Say that unprompted, and mention MinHash or SimHash as the answer if it needed to scale.
